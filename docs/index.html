Can a computer outline a brain tumour?

I trained a computer model to find brain tumours on MRI scans and draw around them, a job doctors do by hand before surgery or radiotherapy. This page puts the model's drawing next to the one a doctor made, so you can see where it gets it right and where it goes wrong. No medical knowledge needed. Details are in the project write-up.

  1. Pick a patient from the list. They range from the model's worst result to a typical one.
  2. Drag the slider under the scan to move through the head, one thin slice at a time. The front of the head is at the top.
  3. Compare the colours. White line: the doctor's outline. Blue: the model's guess. Red: where the model is unsure.
  4. Read the scores beside the scan: how well the two outlines match, and how big the model thinks the tumour is.

What do these words mean?
MRI scan
A 3D picture of the inside of the head, made with magnets instead of X-rays. It is stored as a stack of thin slices, which is what the slider moves through.
Scan 1 (FLAIR) and Scan 2 (with dye)
Two kinds of MRI taken of the same patient. In FLAIR the tumour and the swelling around it look bright. In the dye scan the most active part of the tumour lights up. The model looks at both at once.
Overlap score (Dice)
How much the model's outline and the doctor's outline cover the same area, from 0 (no overlap) to 1 (identical). Two doctors outlining the same tumour typically agree to about 0.75 to 0.85 (Menze et al. 2015).
Three trained copies
The same model was trained three times from different random starting points. Where the three copies give different answers, the model is unsure, and that is shown in red.
Flagged for review
The 10% of patients where the copies disagree most. The model cannot know when it is wrong, but disagreement is a good warning sign: the flag catches more than half of its worst results.
Size range
The model's estimate of the tumour's volume, given as a range rather than one number. Ranges built this way contain the true size for about 9 patients out of 10. For scale, a teaspoon holds 5 mL and a golf ball about 40 mL.
Planted tumour
A real tumour cut out of one patient's scan, shrunk, and blended into another patient's scan. Because its size is chosen, it shows how the model copes with small tumours, which are rare in this dataset.
Images: Medical Segmentation Decathlon, Task01 BrainTumour (BraTS 2016/2017), CC BY-SA 4.0. The size range is a split-conformal 90% interval calibrated on 53 validation patients. Planted tumours are real tumours from validation patients, rescaled and blended into another patient's scan. Not for clinical use.
